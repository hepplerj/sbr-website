The definition of regional boundaries is always a fraught question---and at times pretty academic. The boundary between what constitutes the Midwest and what constitutes the Northern Great Plains perhaps doesn't greatly matter in the scheme of things. Yet these definitions *do* matter for people: we derive identity from our relationship to a place, whether a city or a state or a region or a country. Even for the sake of this project, it's helpful to delineate what I see as the scope of the research I've undertaken.

One challenge about boundaries of place is to determine what constitutes that boundary. Is it a matter of environment and climate? Politics? Culture? Historical experiences? A combination of all?

If as Yi-Fu Tuan suggests that place is a space that's been given meaning and borders by people, region joins this configuration by pulling in relationships to land and landscape. Any attempt to impose a perception of a place is, by it's nature, a relationship of power: setting the meaning of a place privileges one mental concept of the same physical space over another. 

A *sense of region*, then, could be thought of as an *assemblage of places* that share relationships. The *relationships*---plural---are important, because otherwise there is a risk at a kind of regional determinism. This kind of regional determinism was common in the 19th century. Other western boosters and geographical determinists---Frederick Jackson Turner or Walter Prescott Webb---the interaction between environment and inhabitation were the most forceful determinants for a region's identity. 

Webb's *The Great Plains* published in 1931 remains a canonical---and deeply flawed---work. Webb argued that "the Great Plains environment . . . constitutes a geographic unity" that originated at the eastern edge of the 98th meridian[^1] that he called an "institutional fault" where "practically every institution that was carried across it was either broken and remade or else greatly altered." Webb saw the region defined by three key characteristics: it was level, treeless, and semiarid (receiving less than 20 inches of rainfall annually), stretching from the eastern edge to the Pacific slope of the Rockies from Canada to Mexico. Importantly, Webb saw technological change as key in the region: barbed wire, the revolver, and the windmill were emblematic adaptations to this semiarid environment. 

But this sort of environmental determinism doesn't hold up very well (nor did it hold up well at it's time, most sharply rebuked by Fred Shannon in 1940). Yet geography and climate remain an essential way for scholars and residents to define the region. Early twentieth century geographers such as William Morris Davis, Isaiah Bowman, and Nevin Fenneman adopted the phrase "Great Plains" in their work. Fenneman's "Physiographic Subdivision of the United States," published in 1916, remained---and remains---an authoritative understanding of the region rooted in a three tiered classification. Fenneman defined a "Great Plains province" situated between the Rocky Mountains and the Central Lowland, Canadian Shield, and Gulf Coastal Plain to the east stretching north from the Canadian Prairies to the Edwards Plateau in Texas. Fenneman didn't set down a single geographic "Great Plains" but a set of physical escarpments that he divided into the High Plains, the Plains Border, and the Missouri Plateau.

But the region's definition was far from settled. A key influence in the region's definition has been it grasses. University of Nebraska plant ecologist Frederic Clements advanced a theory of climatic "climax" in the first half of the twentieth century, suggesting that vegetation in a climatic zone develops towards a single, stable state. For the Plains, it was the grass. His grassland framework suggested a coherent, natural unit. Historian James C. Malin took this ecology into the field of history. Malin's *The Grassland of North America* (1947) treated the grasslands as a single unit of analysis, insisting that ecology and history worked in parallel. He pioneered the use of farm level Census data and ecological readings of the Plains that anticipated history's move into environmental history---suggesting a vegetation tradition that pushed the region towards a grasslands biome as a whole.

As geographer David Wishart has noted, the Great Plains are "both a real place and an intellectual concept." Instead of a easily defined eastern line, Wishart suggests a combination of physical, historical, and geopolitical critera that led him to draw a line along the eastern borders of North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, and Kansas. He took the western boundary to the Rocky Mountains foothills, north to the Parkland Belt boreal forests, and the Balcones Escarpment and the Rio Grande. Wishart suggests a pragmatic solution: boundaries "which, if not hard and fast, are logical."


[^1]: Wallace Stegner, John Wesley Powell, and others, of course, suggest that the American West begins along the 100th meridian---indicating a regional slippage over using imagined lines on a map.
