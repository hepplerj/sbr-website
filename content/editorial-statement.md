## Scope and selection

*[Editorial cue: open with what this archive is and what it isn't. The first sentence should tell the reader what they're looking at: an annotated archive of primary sources relevant to the recurring sagebrush rebellions and the long history of federal land policy in the American West. Then describe selection criteria — what makes it in. Public laws, court filings, sponsor letters, hearing transcripts, executive orders. What stays out — secondary scholarship (that's the [bibliography](/sources/)), oral histories without permission, etc. A short paragraph; readers should leave knowing both what's here and what's deliberately not.]*

## Sourcing and permissions

*[Editorial cue: how documents arrive in the archive. Public-domain government works are the easy case (federal statutes, federal court opinions, congressional hearings, federal executive orders). For materials you've transcribed from private collections or that may have residual rights questions, note the principle: nothing in the archive is included without either clear public-domain status or explicit permission from the rights-holder. If you're using any materials under fair use, name them and explain the rationale. The Bundy court filings, for instance, are PACER documents that are public records but have copyright complications worth surfacing.]*

## Transcription practices

*[Editorial cue: this is the conventional heart of any editorial statement. The questions readers expect answered:

- Do you preserve the original spelling and punctuation, or silently modernize?
- How do you handle OCR errors in scanned sources — silently correct, mark with `[sic]`, or leave as transcribed?
- How are line breaks, page numbers, and original formatting indicated (or not)?
- For typeset materials, are you preserving small-caps, italics, font shifts?
- For handwritten materials, how do you handle illegible passages — `[illegible]`, conjectural readings in brackets, etc.?
- For deletions, insertions, marginalia in the original — how represented?

The current FLPMA § 102 transcription preserves the Cornell LII text verbatim with original capitalization and punctuation. You'll want to articulate a general principle that scales to other documents.]*

## Annotation conventions

*[Editorial cue: explain the two annotation modes:

- **Glosses** (highlighted spans + marginal sidenotes) — short editorial commentary providing context, cross-references, definitions, or interpretive flagging at the level of a phrase or sentence. Visible in the document body as a rust-underlined span; full text appears in the right-rail sidenote column on wide screens, or as an inline expander on narrow screens.

- **Footnotes** (numbered references at the bottom of the document) — longer analytical commentary, source criticism, comparison with related scholarship, or material that's too lengthy for marginalia.

Then articulate the editorial *voice* of the annotations: scholarly but not didactic, historically situated, citing where claims are contested. The annotations represent your editorial reading, not the documents themselves; readers should be able to distinguish the source from the apparatus at a glance.]*

## Citing items in this archive

*[Editorial cue: give readers the citation form to use. Probably something like: "Jason Heppler, ed., '*Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976 § 102 (Declaration of Policy)*,' *Governing Ground: Documents* (May 2026), https://governingground.org/documents/flpma-1976/." Note that the citation distinguishes the document from your editorial work.]*

## Licensing

*[Editorial cue: state the rights position. The underlying documents are public domain (in the federal-government case) or used with permission. Your editorial contribution — transcription decisions, annotations, the framework of the archive — should have an explicit license. Probably CC BY 4.0 or CC BY-ND 4.0 to match the rest of the site. Clarify that re-users should distinguish your editorial work from the underlying documents.]*

## Versioning and corrections

*[Editorial cue: how the archive handles updates. Every substantive change to a transcription or annotation gets logged in the [changelog](/updates/). Errors reported by readers are corrected and noted. Annotations may be revised as scholarship develops; the changelog reflects that. Optionally include a contact method for corrections — though probably less formal than a "preferred contact" line.]*
