Many of these terms are technical in one community (ranchers, range scientists, federal land managers, historians of the West) and opaque outside it. The brief definitions here are meant for general readers; specialists will know there's more to say.

## Cattle and livestock

### Animal unit month (AUM)
The forage one mature cow with calf (or one horse, or five sheep or goats) needs for one month. The basic unit federal grazing permits and fees are denominated in — a permit might authorize, say, 1,200 AUMs on a given allotment in a given season. The 2024 federal grazing fee was set at the statutory minimum of $1.35 per AUM.

### Beef cow
A mature female bovine kept for breeding in a beef-cattle (rather than dairy) operation. The breeding herd's size is the key supply-side number in cattle economics: cows produce calves, calves become next year's slaughter cattle. When prices fall, ranchers cull cows; when prices rise, they retain heifers to expand the herd.

### Brand
A permanent ownership mark, traditionally hot-iron-burned into the hide. Brand inspection — confirming that the cattle a person is selling belong to them — remains a state-level legal apparatus in much of the West.

### Bull
An intact (unaltered) adult male bovine, kept for breeding.

### Calf
A bovine of either sex under approximately one year old. "Weaned calves" — typically 6–10 months old, 400–600 lbs — are the main income event for a cow-calf operation: they're sold off the range to feeder operations or feedlots.

### Cow
A mature female bovine that has produced at least one calf. (A female bovine that has not yet calved is a *heifer*.)

### Cow-calf operation
The rancher's-side end of the cattle business: maintaining a breeding herd of cows on grass, calving each spring, and selling weaned calves in the fall. The dominant business model on western federal-land grazing allotments.

### Cwt (hundredweight)
A hundred pounds. The conventional unit for quoting cattle prices: "$220/cwt" means $2.20 per pound of liveweight cattle. A 1,200-lb finished steer at $220/cwt is worth $2,640.

### Feedlot
A concentrated operation that finishes cattle on grain to market weight (typically 1,200–1,400 lbs) over 100–200 days. Distinct from the grass-based cow-calf operations that supply it. Concentrated in the southern Plains and corn-belt fringe.

### Finishing
The grain-feeding phase that takes a yearling steer or heifer from grass weight (~700–800 lbs) to slaughter weight (1,200–1,400 lbs).

### Head
A single animal. "Two hundred head of cattle." A common count unit alongside "AUM" and "cwt."

### Heifer
A young female bovine that has not yet produced a calf. Heifers retained from the herd's own calf crop replace older cows as breeding stock; heifers sold for slaughter are part of the same fed-cattle stream as steers.

### Herd
The collective set of cattle a rancher manages — sometimes specifically the breeding herd (cows + bulls + replacement heifers), sometimes the operation's whole inventory.

### Stocking rate
The number of animal units a piece of range can support without degradation, usually expressed in AUMs per acre. The single most-contested number in federal-rancher disputes: when the BLM cuts a permit's stocking rate, the rancher's annual income on that allotment drops.

### Steer
A male bovine castrated young and raised for beef. Steers and heifers make up most of the slaughter cattle stream.

## Range and grazing

### Allotment
A specific named parcel of federal range on which a rancher (or a grazing association) holds a permit to graze a specific class and number of livestock for a specific season. The administrative atom of federal-range politics — every meaningful BLM grazing decision (stocking cuts, closures, NEPA review) happens at this scale. The BLM administers roughly 21,700 allotments nationally; the USFS another 8,400.

### Animal unit month
See *AUM*.

### Checkerboard
The alternating-section ownership pattern produced by 19th-century railroad land grants: odd-numbered sections to the railroad, even-numbered to the public domain. The "literal checkerboard" still visible on land-ownership maps across the West — and the source of much in-holding complexity in modern federal-land administration.

### Deferred grazing
A range-management practice of resting a pasture from grazing for part or all of a season to allow vegetation recovery. One of the principal tools the BLM uses (along with stocking cuts) to address overgrazing.

### Free range / open range
The pre-1934 condition: federal public domain on which any rancher's cattle could graze without permit, fee, or allotment. Ended (in the conterminous US) by the Taylor Grazing Act of 1934.

### Grazing district
The administrative unit one level above the allotment. The Taylor Grazing Act of 1934 organized the public range into grazing districts (originally Department of Grazing, then Grazing Service, now BLM); each district contains many allotments and is staffed by its own BLM office.

### Grazing fee
The per-AUM price the federal government charges permittees to graze on BLM and USFS land. Set by a 1978 statutory formula that ties the fee to private-lease rates and cattle prices, but with a $1.35/AUM floor that has been binding for most years since the early 1980s. A perennial controversy: ranchers consider it part of the cost of doing business; critics consider it a subsidy because it sits well below private-lease rates.

### In-holding
A parcel of private (or state, or tribal) land surrounded by federal land, usually a survival of the railroad-land-grant checkerboard, the Homestead Act, or earlier mining patents. Complicates federal-land management because routes, water sources, and grazing patterns may cross the parcel.

### Multiple use
The statutory mandate (Multiple-Use Sustained-Yield Act, 1960; later FLPMA, 1976) that federal rangelands and forests be managed for a balance of grazing, timber, mineral leasing, recreation, watershed protection, and wildlife habitat — not for a single purpose. Sagebrush-rebellion arguments often hinge on which uses an agency is privileging at a given moment.

### Permit
The formal authorization that lets a rancher graze a specified number of animals on a specified allotment for a specified season. Ten-year renewable terms are typical. Permits are tied to (and transferable with) "base property" — private deeded land the operator owns nearby.

### Public domain
Historically, the federal land that had passed to the United States from foreign sovereigns (treaty, purchase, or cession) and not yet been transferred to private, state, or tribal ownership. Most western states still hold substantial public domain; the Taylor Grazing Act (1934) and FLPMA (1976) effectively ended the disposal era and committed the remaining public domain to permanent federal retention.

### Range
The unimproved, mostly-untilled land — grassland, sagebrush steppe, desert scrub — on which cattle and sheep graze. Most of the federal-land West is range.

### Rest rotation
A grazing-management system that takes part of an allotment out of use each year on rotation, so each pasture gets a full year (or longer) of rest periodically. One of the more rigorous BLM range-management approaches.

### Sustained yield
Companion to *multiple use*: the principle that federal lands should be managed so the resources they produce (forage, timber, etc.) can continue indefinitely. Originally a Forest Service concept (Pinchot's "wise use"); FLPMA extended it to BLM lands.

## Federal lands and land measurement

### Acre
43,560 square feet, or 1/640 of a square mile. The basic unit of US land measurement, fixed in colonial-era English statute and inherited through the 1785 Land Ordinance.

### Disposal
Transfer of federal land out of federal ownership — to private homesteaders (Homestead Act, Desert Land Act), to states (statehood enabling acts, Pittman Act), to railroads (Pacific Railway Acts), or to mining claimants. The disposal era effectively ended with the 1934 Taylor Grazing Act and was statutorily closed for BLM lands by FLPMA in 1976.

### Federal estate
The total federal land holding, ~640 million acres or roughly 28% of the US. Distributed among the BLM (~245M acres), USFS (~193M), FWS (~89M wildlife refuges), and NPS (~80M parks and monuments), plus smaller holdings by DOD, BOR, BIA, and others.

### Land grant
A grant of federal public-domain land — to states (Morrill Act of 1862 funded land-grant universities), to railroads (Pacific Railway Acts 1862–1871 distributed ~175M acres), or to individual settlers (Homestead Act).

### Monument (national monument)
A federal-land designation under the Antiquities Act of 1906, proclaimed by the president without Congressional consent, intended to protect objects of historic or scientific interest. Most administered by NPS; some by BLM or USFS. The site of the most contested presidential land-use authority — Bears Ears (2016, reduced 2017, restored 2021) is the recent flashpoint.

### National forest
Land administered by the US Forest Service (USDA). The system began with the Forest Reserve Act of 1891 and now totals ~193 million acres across 154 forests and 20 grasslands. Managed under the multiple-use / sustained-yield framework.

### National grassland
USFS-administered grassland created from former Bankhead-Jones Farm Tenant Act (1937) submarginal-farmland acquisitions, formally designated as grasslands by Secretary Benson in 1960. ~3.8 million acres, mostly on the Plains.

### National park
Land set aside by Congress (not just presidential proclamation) and administered by the National Park Service under the 1916 Organic Act. The most preservation-oriented federal-land designation; commercial uses like grazing, mining, and timber harvest are tightly restricted.

### Quarter section
160 acres — one-fourth of a section. The standard Homestead Act parcel. "Quarter-quarter section" = 40 acres.

### Section
One square mile (640 acres). The basic survey unit of the federal rectangular cadastral system established by the 1785 Land Ordinance. Each township contains 36 numbered sections.

### Surface Management Agency (SMA)
A categorization that assigns every parcel of federal land to its primary administering agency (BLM, USFS, NPS, FWS, etc.). The BLM publishes the canonical SMA polygon dataset.

### Township
A six-mile-square block (36 square miles) in the federal rectangular survey, the level above sections. Townships are numbered by their distance north or south of a baseline and east or west of a meridian.

### Wilderness area
A federal-land designation under the 1964 Wilderness Act: lands kept "untrammeled by man," with no roads, motorized vehicles, mechanized equipment, timber harvest, or new mining claims. Designated by Congress (not the executive). About 112 million acres nationwide; the BLM, USFS, NPS, and FWS each administer wilderness on their respective lands.

### Wilderness Study Area (WSA)
Federal land inventoried as potentially eligible for wilderness designation, managed under interim wilderness-like protections pending Congressional action. FLPMA (1976) ordered the BLM inventory; many WSAs have been waiting on Congress for decades.

### Withdrawal
Removal of a parcel of federal land from one set of public-land laws (typically the Mining Law of 1872, the Mineral Leasing Act, or homestead-era disposal laws) by executive or congressional action. The administrative tool by which national monuments, wildlife refuges, and many other special-use designations were originally established.

## Major statutes and policies

### Antiquities Act (1906)
Authorizes the president to proclaim national monuments by executive order. The single most-contested presidential public-lands authority of the past century.

### ANILCA (Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act, 1980)
Withdrew 104 million acres of Alaska into conservation-system units, doubling the national park system. The largest single federal land conservation action in US history.

### Bankhead-Jones Farm Tenant Act (1937)
Title III authorized federal acquisition of "submarginal" farmland during the Dust Bowl, eventually producing the National Grasslands and Land Utilization Projects.

### ESA (Endangered Species Act, 1973)
Federal protection for threatened and endangered species and their habitat. A recurring constraint on grazing, mining, and oil-and-gas activity on federal land, and a primary legislative target of the 1990s–2000s Wise Use movement.

### FLPMA (Federal Land Policy and Management Act, 1976)
The BLM's organic act. Establishes the agency's mission to retain and manage public lands in federal ownership for multiple use — the retention clause is the precise premise the 1979 Sagebrush Rebellion challenged.

### Homestead Act (1862)
Granted 160 acres of public domain to any settler who improved it for five years; distributed about 270 million acres over the act's lifetime.

### Mining Law (General Mining Law of 1872)
Allows US citizens to stake mining claims on most federal land, with patenting rights to subsurface minerals at $2.50–$5.00 per acre. Substantively unchanged since the Grant administration.

### MUSY (Multiple-Use Sustained-Yield Act, 1960)
Codified multiple use as the statutory mandate for national forests.

### NEPA (National Environmental Policy Act, 1969)
Requires environmental impact statements for major federal actions. The procedural tool environmental groups have used most heavily to challenge federal-land decisions.

### PILT (Payments in Lieu of Taxes)
Federal payments to counties that contain federal land, compensating them for the property-tax base they can't reach. Enacted 1976 as part of FLPMA, funded annually since 1977. See the [Federal Payments to Western Counties](/sightlines/federal-payments/) chart.

### SRS (Secure Rural Schools Act, 2000)
Replaced much of the collapsed Forest Service 25% revenue-sharing fund with a congressional appropriation, sustaining rural-county budgets after the post-1990 collapse in federal timber receipts.

### Taylor Grazing Act (1934)
Ended the open-range era by establishing federal grazing districts and the permit/allotment system. Created the Grazing Service, which merged with the General Land Office in 1946 to become the BLM.

### Wilderness Act (1964)
Created the National Wilderness Preservation System. The legal structure the sagebrush coalition has spent six decades trying to unwind.

## Agencies and abbreviations

### BIA
Bureau of Indian Affairs (Department of the Interior). Administers tribal trust land — ~55 million surface acres.

### BLM
Bureau of Land Management (Department of the Interior). Administers ~245 million surface acres and ~700 million subsurface mineral acres, mostly in the West. Created in 1946 by merging the General Land Office (1812) and the Grazing Service (1934). The single most-contested land-managing agency in the sagebrush-rebellion story.

### BLS
Bureau of Labor Statistics (Department of Labor). Source of the CPI-U index used to deflate prices on this site's economic charts.

### BOR
Bureau of Reclamation (Department of the Interior), often "USBR." Builds and operates dams and irrigation works in the 17 western states.

### CRS
Congressional Research Service. The non-partisan research arm of Congress; its reports (especially R42346 on federal land ownership) are foundational references for this project.

### DOD
Department of Defense. Holds withdrawal authority over significant tracts for military training ranges and installations.

### EROS
Earth Resources Observation and Science Center (USGS, Sioux Falls SD). Hosts the archived National Atlas data, satellite imagery archives, and other USGS geospatial holdings.

### ERS
Economic Research Service (USDA). Publishes the agricultural-economy statistical series this project draws on, including the historical farm-count and farm-income data.

### FRED
Federal Reserve Economic Data, published by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. Free CSV downloads, no key required — used here for the CPI-U deflator on the cattle-prices chart.

### FWS / USFWS
Fish and Wildlife Service (Department of the Interior). Administers ~150 million acres across 560+ National Wildlife Refuges plus marine reserves.

### GLO
General Land Office. The 1812 predecessor of the BLM, responsible for surveying and disposing of the public domain. Merged with the Grazing Service to form the BLM in 1946. GLO survey-plat records are the primary source for the historical cadastral grid.

### GPO
Government Publishing Office. Publishes the daily *Federal Register* and bulk legislative data (BILLSTATUS XML) used in this project's cosponsorship-network pipeline.

### NASS
National Agricultural Statistics Service (USDA). Operates the QuickStats API used here to fetch farm counts, average farm size, and cattle prices.

### NCEI
National Centers for Environmental Information (NOAA). Operates the "Climate at a Glance" national and regional time-series products used for this project's temperature and precipitation charts.

### NOAA
National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (Department of Commerce). Parent of NCEI and the National Weather Service; administers marine national monuments.

### NPS
National Park Service (Department of the Interior). Administers ~80 million acres of parks, monuments, and historic sites.

### PAD-US
Protected Areas Database of the United States. The federal authoritative dataset of land-protection categories, compiled by USGS GAP. Successor to the National Atlas land-protection layers.

### USDA
US Department of Agriculture. Cabinet department containing the Forest Service, NASS, ERS, and other agricultural-policy units.

### USFS
US Forest Service (USDA). Administers ~193 million acres across 154 national forests and 20 national grasslands.

## Movements and politics

### Bundy standoff
The April 2014 armed confrontation at the Bundy Ranch outside Bunkerville, Nevada, after the BLM attempted to impound Cliven Bundy's cattle for unpaid grazing fees accumulated since 1993. Federal agents withdrew without completing the impoundment. The standoff energized the patriot-movement coalition that would carry the Malheur occupation two years later.

### County supremacy
A 1990s legal-political movement asserting that federal lands within a county's boundaries are subject to county-level land-use ordinances, with federal management subordinate. *U.S. v. Nye County* (Nevada, 1996) was the test case; the court ruled against the county-supremacy theory.

### Malheur occupation
The 41-day armed takeover of the Malheur National Wildlife Refuge in Harney County, Oregon, in January–February 2016, led by Ammon Bundy. Triggered by the resentencing of the Hammond family on federal arson charges related to range fires that crossed onto BLM land. Federal trials produced mixed verdicts.

### Oath Keepers
A patriot-movement organization founded in 2009, recruiting current and former law-enforcement and military members under a pledge to refuse "unconstitutional" orders. Prominent at Bunkerville (2014) and at the Malheur occupation (2016).

### Patriot movement
The loose coalition of militia groups, sovereign-citizen advocates, and constitutional-restorationist organizations that emerged in the 1990s and reactivated in the late 2000s. Adjacent to (and sometimes overlapping with) the sagebrush-rebellion coalition; Bunkerville and Malheur are the most-public points of intersection.

### Sagebrush Rebellion
Both a specific 1979–82 episode (Nevada Assembly Bill 413 asserting state ownership over BLM land, with companion legislation in Utah, Wyoming, Arizona, New Mexico) and a broader recurring pattern of western-state resistance to federal land authority. Historians sometimes identify additional waves — the 1946–48 livestock-industry transfer push and the 2010s land-transfer movement under the [American Lands Council](https://americanlandscouncil.org/) — as further episodes.

### Transfer (of public lands)
The shorthand for any proposal that federal land in the western states be transferred to state ownership and management. The core policy demand of the sagebrush coalition since 1946; no transfer of substantial federal land has actually occurred.

### Wise Use movement
The institutional coalition that bridged the 1979 Sagebrush Rebellion and the 1990s county-supremacy fights. Launched by Ron Arnold and Alan Gottlieb at a 1988 Reno conference, organized around property-rights, multiple-use, and anti-environmental themes.
